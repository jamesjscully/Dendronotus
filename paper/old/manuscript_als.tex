\begin{abstract}


The swim central pattern generator (CPG) of Dendronotus iris produces robust rhythmic activity despite variability in its components and external perturbations.
This study examines the contributions of slow synaptic dynamics and the coexistence of multiple oscillatory pathways to the resilience of this network.
We constructed a four-cell model based on experimental data, incorporating slow synaptic processes and fast-slow decomposition.
Preliminary findings indicate that robustness emerges from the combined influence of multiple oscillatory mechanisms rather than reliance on a single dominant pathway.
This model provides a framework for understanding adaptive motor rhythms and their application to biological and synthetic systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Animal locomotion emerges from neural circuits that must remain effective despite intrinsic variability, changing body size, and fluctuating environmental conditions.
Early work on locusts demonstrated ``fictive locomotion,'' revealing that rhythmic motor output can persist without sensory feedback \cite{Wilson1961}.
This led to the identification of autonomous, rhythmogenic circuits known as central pattern generators (CPGs) that drive locomotion in both invertebrates and vertebrates \cite{MarderCalabrese1996, Kiehn2006}.


Because locomotion underpins critical behaviors like foraging and predator avoidance, CPGs evolve under selective pressure to adapt to environmental variables like temperature and maintain appropriate scaling among body size, locomotion speed, and rhythmic frequency \cite{Marder2023}.
These circuits integrate sensory feedback and descending control signals, relying on dynamical mechanisms across multiple timescales: spikes at milliseconds, bursts shaped over seconds, and homeostatic feedback stabilizing circuits over minutes to days \cite{MarderCalabrese1996}.

Prior research has shown that ion channel degeneracy allows broad variability in conductances among genetically identical individuals to yield similar functional states \cite{Marder2011, OLeary2014}, with homeostatic feedback linked to intracellular calcium stabilizing neuronal activity \cite{Franci2020}.
However, this does not explain how synapses and cells coordinate to produce reliable bursting across a range of frequencies.
We propose that controllability, the ability to produce diverse behaviors by modulating a control variable, is another key factor.
In this study, we demonstrate how the kinetic properties of synaptic enhancement allow burst frequency control in the \textit{Dendronotus iris} swim CPG \cite{SakuraiKatz2016}.

Initially driven by evolutionary questions about locomotor diversity within its clade---including dorsal-ventral swimmers, left-right swimmers, and non-swimmers \cite{Newcomb2012}---our research on the \textit{Dendronotus iris} swim CPG uncovered a novel role for neuroplasticity.
Our modeling indicates that the temporal properties of synaptic enhancement, rather than its magnitude or static strength, confer precise control over the network's bursting frequency and pattern [neuroplasticity citation].
Specifically, the kinetics of synaptic enhancement provide network-level adaptability in this system \cite{SakuraiKatz2016}.

Looking ahead, this work highlights the critical role of synaptic plasticity kinetics in governing neural circuit dynamics [neuroplasticity citation].
These properties may serve as a general mechanism for controllability across diverse systems, though the biological processes regulating them remain underexplored.
Synaptic enhancement spans facilitation, augmentation, and post-tetanic potentiation, with augmentation matching the timescale of bursting in the \textit{Dendronotus iris} CPG [neuroplasticity citation].
This could involve co-regulation of enhancement forms or selective modulation of augmentation, possibly via synaptotagmin isoforms [neuroplasticity citation].

The paper is organized as follows: First, we describe the swim CPG network assembly, detailing neuron and synaptic interaction modeling.
Second, we validate the network by comparing simulated voltage traces with empirical data from laboratory experiments involving current injections and perturbations.
Finally, we explore how burst frequency is precisely controlled through synaptic enhancement kinetics, demonstrating the pivotal role of intermediate timescale dynamics in adaptive locomotion.

The central objective of this study is to develop and validate a computational model of the \textit{Dendronotus iris} swim central pattern generator (CPG) that enhances network robustness through a four-cell circuit.
This was achieved by constructing the model stepwise, beginning with well-characterized two-cell subnetworks and integrating them into a larger network.
A systematic approach ensured biological plausibility, dynamic stability, and alignment with empirical data.
Detailed analysis of the neuronal, synaptic, and subnetwork models, including fast-slow decomposition and two-cell oscillatory subnetwork construction, is provided in \cite{scully2024pairing}.
We summarize the essential details here for convenience.

\subsection*{Neuronal Model}
We adapted a conductance-based swim interneuron (SiN) model from Plant's 1981 framework \cite{plant81, scully2024pairing}, modifying it with an additional fast $I_h$ current to represent the cellular components of the \textit{Dendronotus iris} CPG.
The governing equation is:
\begin{equation}
C_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -I_{Na} - I_{Ca} - I_K - I_\text{leak} - I_h - I_{KCa},
\end{equation}
where $C_m$ (1 $\mu$F/cm$^2$) is the membrane capacitance, $V$ is the membrane voltage, and ionic currents include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fast inward current}: A combined sodium-calcium current with instantaneous activation and slow inactivation, akin to Hodgkin-Huxley, driving action potentials.

    \item \textbf{Slow inward current}: A TTX-resistant current with slow activation, contributing to bursting periodicity.
    \item \textbf{Fast outward current}: A potassium current, Hodgkin-Huxley-like, for repolarization.
    \item \textbf{Slow outward current}: A calcium-sensitive potassium current, tied to slow intracellular calcium buildup, shaping burst duration.
    \item \textbf{Leak current}: A passive inward current for baseline dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Fast hyperpolarization-activated current}: $I_h$, added to enhance dynamics at hyperpolarized voltages.
\end{itemize}

The full description of the model equations are given in Ref.~\cite{scully2024pairing}, see also the Appendix~I below.

Slow variables, intracellular calcium concentration $[\rm Ca]$ and gating variable $x$, evolve as:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{x_\infty(V) - x}{\tau_x},\quad 
\frac{d[\rm Ca]}{dt} = \rho \left( K_c x (E_{Ca} - V + \Delta { [\rm Ca]}) - [\rm Ca] \right),
\end{equation}
where $x_\infty(V)$ and $\tau_x$ (ms) are voltage-dependent activation and time constants, and $\rho$ (ms$^{-1}$) and $K_c$ (mM$^{-1}$) control calcium influx and decay.

The parameter $\Delta { [\rm Ca]}$ controls the intrinsic excitability of the neuron. Decreasing it is equivalent to increasing the cell's excitability.
The parameter $\Delta _{x}$ changes how the cell responds qualitatively to synaptic drive. When $\Delta _{x}$ is 0, the model has an endogenous bursting regime. Decreasing $\Delta _{x}$ to -4 mV eliminates this endogenous bursting.

\subsection*{Synaptic Models}
Synaptic dynamics are modelled using a phenomenological approach detailed in \cite{scully2024pairing}, which balances realism and tractability.
Two synapse types were implemented:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fast synapses}: Modeled with a gating variable $S$ that rises quickly with presynaptic voltage and decays at a fixed rate ($\alpha = 10$ ms$^{-1}$, $\beta = 5$ ms$^{-1}$), following the $\alpha$-synapse formulation \cite{destexhe1994synthesis}.
    \item \textbf{Slow synapses}: Include a nonlinear term for synaptic accumulation (minima above zero) and high-pass filtering of low-frequency inputs.
\end{enumerate}
Full equations are in \cite{scully2024pairing} or the appendix.

\subsection*{Four-Cell Network Assembly}
We constructed the four-cell network using validated two-cell or pairwise subnetworks 
such as half-center oscillators 
(HCOs) and  excitatory-inhibitory (EI) modules introduced in Ref.~\cite{scully2024pairing}.
 
 
Synaptic kinetics for mutual inhibition matched HCO results, and E/I interactions were similarly aligned.

We preserved cellular parameters and synaptic kinetics, adjusting only the endogenous excitability of Si3 (swim interneuron 3) by shifting its voltage threshold $\Delta [\rm Ca]$ by 5mV to enhance spiking.
Calibration relied on symmetrically tuning synaptic conductances, following these steps:\\
%
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Configuration.} Substituted pre- and postsynaptic voltage variables to reflect the known \textit{Dendronotus iris} circuit \cite{SakuraiKatz2016}.
    \item \textbf{Initialization.} Set all cells (Si1--Si4) to a quiescent state and synaptic conductances to zero.
    \item \textbf{Drive.} Applied synaptic drive to Si3 until the excitatory postsynaptic $S$
    accumulated with minima above 0.1, saturating over 0.5--3 s (1/4 to 1/2 the burst period
     of 2--12s, varying with animal size).
    \item \textbf{Excitation.} Increased excitatory conductance from Si3 to contralateral Si2 until inhibitory $S$ (Si2-to-Si2) accumulated with minima above 0.1 over 0.5--3 s.
    \item \textbf{Slow Inhibition.} Raised E/I pair inhibitory conductance until Si3 frequency dropped by $50\%$ over 1 s.
    \item \textbf{Fast Inhibition.} Elevated inhibitory conductances for both inhibitory HCOs (Si1--Si2 and Si3--Si5) until a stable burst rhythm emerged, visually resembling empirical voltage traces \cite{SakuraiKatz2016}.
    \item \textbf{Fine-Tuning.} Adjusted excitability slightly (e.g., $\Delta [Ca] < 2$ mV) to set a 30\% duty cycle, verified as extrinsic by resetting conductances to zero.
\end{enumerate}
