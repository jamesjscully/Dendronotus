\subsection{Top-down control and neuromodulation}

In the biological circuit, Si3 neurons are not intrinsically oscillatory but receive tonic excitatory drive from Si1 command neurons that initiate and sustain swim episodes \cite{sakurai2019command}.
We now examine a five-cell network configuration that incorporates Si1 control, with Si3 neurons set to quiescent baseline states that require Si1 excitation to become active.

\subsubsection{Si1 gating of network activity}

In \textit{Dendronotus iris}, Si1 neurons function as command-like neurons that initiate and terminate swim episodes \cite{sakurai2019command}.
They provide tonic excitatory drive to both Si3 neurons, which in turn activate the four-cell CPG through the contralateral E/I modules.
This hierarchical organization allows Si1 neurons to gate network state transitions while intrinsic CPG dynamics shape burst structure and left-right alternation.

Figure~\ref{fig:si1_neuromod}A--C summarizes the biological and modeling context for Si1-mediated control.
Panel A shows the network architecture: Si1 provides excitatory drive to both Si3 neurons and modulates the Si3$\to$Si2 excitatory pathway.
Panel B shows a representative biological Si1/Si2 burst from preparation 130618-03, illustrating that elevated Si1 firing coincides with Si2 bursting.
Panel C shows a representative model trace from the presynaptic modulation regime with the same qualitative sequence: Si1/V0 spiking rises first and Si2 bursting follows.
Together these panels motivate treating Si1 activity as both a gating signal and a frequency-control signal.

\subsubsection{Neuromodulatory burst frequency control}

Beyond gating network activity, Si1 neurons regulate burst frequency in an activity-dependent manner.
Experimental recordings demonstrate that swim frequency increases with Si1 firing rate, establishing that the command signal encodes not merely on/off state but graded frequency information \cite{sakurai2019command}.
This observation motivates our exploration of how neuromodulation of synaptic properties in the Si3$\to$Si2 excitatory pathway could implement such graded control.
We compared two distinct mechanisms to determine which provides appropriate frequency dynamics.

Figure~\ref{fig:si1_neuromod}D--F presents the neuromodulation comparison.
Panel D shows presynaptic modulation, where the Si1-driven modulatory variable affects the accumulation rate parameter $\alpha$ of the logistic Si3$\to$Si2 excitatory synapse.
This produces smooth, graded changes in burst frequency that scale predictably with modulatory input strength.

Panel E shows the corresponding post-drive behavior.
Presynaptic modulation shuts the rhythm off when the drive is withdrawn, whereas postsynaptic modulation tends to leave the network in a persistent bursting state.

Panel F compares the biological relationship between Si1 firing rate and burst frequency with matched model measurements.
The presynaptic model spans the observed trend more naturally, while the postsynaptic model remains steeper and more weakly terminating.

Presynaptic modulation provides smooth, stable frequency control appropriate for an adaptable locomotor CPG that must accommodate developmental scaling and environmental adjustments.
Postsynaptic modulation's high sensitivity may be less suitable for sustained swimming but could enable rapid behavioral state switching.
These computational findings generate a testable prediction: if Si1-driven frequency control operates through presynaptic mechanisms, pharmacological manipulation of release machinery should produce graded frequency changes, whereas manipulation of postsynaptic receptors should produce more abrupt transitions.
Such experiments would distinguish between these modulatory strategies and inform our understanding of how command neurons regulate CPG output.
