\section*{SUMMARY}

Rhythmic behaviors such as swimming rely on central pattern generators (CPGs) that must be robust to perturbation yet tunable by descending commands.
Early work showed that some CPGs function as ``network oscillators,'' generating rhythm through synaptic interactions rather than through dedicated intrinsic pacemaker neurons alone \cite{Getting1983delayed, Getting1989}.
Although pacemaker-driven and half-center mechanisms have been extensively studied, the principles by which network oscillators achieve graded frequency control remain less well characterized.

We address this question through computational modeling of the swim CPG of the nudibranch \textit{Dendronotus iris}, a four-neuron core circuit controlled by Si1 command neurons.
We identify contralateral excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) modules as the central rhythmogenic element of the circuit.
These modules operate in an ``almost-oscillatory'' regime in which slow synaptic accumulation creates the history dependence required for network-level state transitions, a mechanism we term \textit{network hysteresis}.

Progressive network assembly shows that these E/I modules, together with reciprocal inhibition between the left and right Si2 and Si3 interneuron pairs, are sufficient to generate robust alternating bursting.
The model reproduces experimental perturbation responses without parameter fitting, supporting the interpretation that rhythm generation is distributed across the circuit rather than localized to a single essential pacemaker neuron.

We further show that gain-dependent modulation of the Si3$\to$Si2 excitatory pathway can tune burst frequency during active drive.
Using the same dimensionless gain parameter in two model variants, we compare presynaptic modulation of synaptic accumulation kinetics with postsynaptic modulation of effective synaptic conductance.
Both implementations provide plausible mechanisms for Si1-linked frequency control while producing different gain-to-frequency relationships.

These findings provide a mechanistic account of how network oscillators can combine resilience with controllability.
More broadly, they suggest that slow synaptic dynamics can serve as the primary substrate for flexible motor-pattern regulation in circuits that lack intrinsic bursting neurons.
