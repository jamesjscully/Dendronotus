# Summary

Rhythmic behaviors like swimming rely on Central Pattern Generators (CPGs) that must be both robust to perturbation and tunable by descending commands.
 While mechanisms for rhythm generation are well-known, it remains unclear how small networks lacking intrinsic bursting neurons achieve smooth, graded frequency control without destabilizing the pattern. We investigate this through computational modeling of the swim CPG of the nudibranch *Dendronotus iris*, a four-neuron circuit where a unified command signal regulates frequency despite the absence of intrinsic pacemakers.

Using a computational model grounded in experimental connectivity, we identify the core rhythmogenic engine as contralateral excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) modules operating near an oscillatory threshold. In this regime, the rhythm arises from network interactions constrained by slow synaptic kinetics that match the burst period, rather than cellular properties. We show that modulating the rate of presynaptic transmitter release provides a smooth, monotonic control mechanism for frequency, whereas modulating postsynaptic conductance yields limited, threshold-like control. The model reproduces experimental responses to perturbations without parameter fitting. These findings establish a circuit-level mechanism whereby synaptic dynamics enable robust yet controllable rhythm generation in minimal networks.
